How it's tested

Under the Digital Services Act, Meta keeps every ad shown in the European Union for a year after it last ran. Each image is read against eighteen fixed questions, from light and setting to mood, and kept only as its answers and a numerical fingerprint. Each house is measured against the controls month by month, so a movement of the whole market doesn't read as a movement of one house.

Three tests were frozen on 5 October 2026, before a single ad was collected.

  • Shift. Did the ten move further after their debuts than the controls moved at placebo dates?
  • Transfer. Did Gucci's advertising move towards Balenciaga's earlier look, following Demna? This is the mechanism: a look that belongs to people should travel with them.
  • Detection. Does a scan that isn't told the dates find the breaks, and stay quiet on the controls?

A prediction, made in advance

Frozen before Saint Laurent named a successor to Anthony Vaccarello: after the successor's first show, its advertising should shift more than nine in ten control placebos, break within 120 days, and move towards the successor's former house if that house is collected. Judged once, 270 days after the show.

What it can't say

It reads paid advertising on Meta's platforms in Europe and Britain, not print or the clothes. Image-makers moved with the designers, so a transfer is read as a team's, not a designer's alone. And it can show that advertising changed, not that the change worked.

Change and attention

The obvious heat measure, the Lyst Index, changed its method in early 2026 and began counting Chanel and Dior, so its rankings either side of the debuts don't compare. Wikipedia page views, counted the same way throughout, stand in as attention: whether bigger shifts drew more of it, not whether they sold more.
